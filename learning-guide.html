Chapter 1 · Biology

What is a protein?

Proteins are molecular machines made from chains of building blocks called amino acids.

Different proteins do different jobs: some speed up chemical reactions, some bind to other molecules, and some glow. Their amino-acid sequence influences how they fold into a three-dimensional shape, which in turn influences what they can do. Environment matters too, so sequence alone does not tell the whole story.

There are 20 standard amino acids, each represented by a letter. For example, M, S, and K represent different amino acids. A protein sequence is their order in the chain. Genes encode proteins, but the sequences in our CSVs are protein sequences, not DNA sequences. [NHGRI glossary]

Check yourself: What does one letter in a protein sequence represent?

One amino acid at one position in the protein chain.

Chapter 2 · Variation

What changes between proteins?

A reference is a sequence chosen as a starting point. It is often called the wild type, but always check which reference a dataset actually uses. A variant is a sequence being compared with it.

Reference   M S K G V F
Variant     M S R G V F
Position    1 2 3 4 5 6

Illustrative example, not a complete protein. At position 3, K was replaced with R: one substitution, written K3R.

Single mutant

One amino acid differs from the reference, such as K3R.

Multiple mutant

Several positions differ, such as K3R:V55A. The colon separates changes.

Mutation distance counts substitutions from the reference when sequences are aligned and the same length. It does not say whether the mutations help or hurt. For different lengths, simple position-by-position Hamming distance is not defined.

Check yourself: What does V55A mean?

The reference amino acid V at position 55 has been replaced by A.

Chapter 3 · Measurement

How does a sequence get a score?

Researchers create or collect variants and use an assay—a laboratory test—to measure a property. A score is a processed measurement of that property. Depending on the experiment, it could reflect fluorescence, binding, or enzyme activity.

Make variantsDifferent protein sequences
→
MeasureRun the assay
→
ProcessConvert measurements to scores
→
AnalyzeCompare variants carefully

Fitness in these datasets means performance according to a particular measurement, not a universal measure of how “good” a protein is. An assay has conditions, measurement noise, and often a limited range. Scores may be transformed or normalized. A negative number is not automatically bad, and scores from different assays are not directly comparable.

Concrete example: GFP is a fluorescent protein. Its dataset measures a fluorescence-related score; ProteinGym documents higher DMS_score as better fitness. The score is not a universal percentage of brightness. [ProteinGym]

High-throughput approaches such as deep mutational scanning can measure many variants in a single experimental workflow. The exact procedure differs by dataset. [Deep mutational scanning overview]

Check yourself: Can a score of −2 in one dataset be compared directly to 2 in another?

No. They can measure different properties and use different transformations or scales. First find out what each score means.

Chapter 4 · The search space

Imagine a landscape of possibilities.

Each possible sequence has a score under a particular assay. Together, the sequences and their scores form a fitness landscape. We cannot usually measure every possible sequence: even a 100-position protein has an enormous number of possible combinations.

Illustration only: sequence space is high-dimensional, not a literal one-dimensional line. Dots represent a few measured variants.

Nearby sequences can behave differently. Two changes may interact: their combined effect need not equal the sum of their individual effects. This interaction is called epistasis. It makes predicting unseen combinations difficult.

Check yourself: Why can’t we simply measure every possible sequence?

The number of possible amino-acid combinations grows enormously with sequence length, and experiments cost time and resources.

Chapter 6 · Read the data

What does an actual row tell us?

Here is a real GFP record from the local CSV. The sequence is shortened for readability; the score and mutation notation are retained.

mutant:            K3Q:V16A:I167T:L195Q
mutated_sequence:  MSQGEELFTGVVPILA…
DMS_score:        3.13735
DMS_score_bin:    1

Mutation description

Four substitutions relative to the dataset’s reference: K→Q at position 3, V→A at 16, I→T at 167, and L→Q at 195.

Sequence

The resulting amino-acid chain. The actual file contains the full sequence; the ellipsis here only abbreviates it.

Continuous score

DMS_score is the measured, processed fitness score. Higher is better for this assay.

Binary label

DMS_score_bin groups variants into two categories. Check upstream documentation before interpreting the exact threshold.

For FLIP files, common columns are sequence, target, set, and validation. target is the score; set identifies train/test membership. A validation flag identifies some training rows reserved for validation. In GB1, blank validation values occur and require source-aware handling.

Check yourself: Does “four mutations” mean the score must be low?

No. It tells us the number of changes, not the score. Some four-mutant sequences can perform well and others poorly.

Chapter 7 · Machine learning

Learning from some examples.

In supervised learning, we give a model sequences and their known scores. It learns a relationship and predicts scores for sequences whose measurements it has not been given.

TrainFit using known examples
→
ValidateChoose settings using separate examples
→
TestEvaluate on held-out examples

To use a sequence in a model, we need a numerical representation: for example, one-hot encoding records which amino acid occurs at each position; an embedding is a learned numerical representation. CERA has not generated embeddings or trained models yet.

Why the split matters

A random split may contain very similar sequences in both train and test. Other splits deliberately create a harder gap. In GB1’s local split, the training examples have scores below 1 and the test examples start at 1. The model must extrapolate from lower scores to higher ones.

The blocks show split membership, not the actual distribution or relative score range.

Important distinction: GB1 and Hydro’s score-based splits were created using measured scores. They are useful retrospective challenges, but this label-based construction is an assumption to disclose. During an experiment, hidden scores and statistics derived from them must not influence the optimizer.

A model may be accurate on average yet wrong about the few sequences it ranks highest. That matters because an optimization system actively searches for high predictions, which can expose weaknesses a typical test score misses.

Check yourself: Why keep test scores hidden during training and selection?

Otherwise the model or experimenter can indirectly use the answers, making evaluation overly optimistic. This is called label leakage.

Chapter 8 · Research hypothesis

Why look for counterexamples?

A proxy is a stand-in for the thing we actually care about. Here a predicted score can stand in for a costly laboratory measurement. A proxy is useful, but it can be wrong.

PredictModel expects high fitness
→
CheckQuery the actual score
→
Find a failurePrediction and measurement disagree
→
Improve?Study whether repair helps

For example, a model predicts 3.8 for a sequence, but the measured score is 1.4. That is a counterexample to the model’s optimistic prediction. The values here are invented to explain the idea, not a CERA result.

CERA proposes to use counterexamples, uncertainty estimates, and constrained repair to study and improve unreliable learned rewards. Whether the approach works must be tested against appropriate baselines, with a fixed query budget and hidden labels. In a retrospective benchmark, a held-out measured score can act as a simulated oracle; it is not a new lab measurement.

Current status: The research loop, oracle/query logic, models, and reward repair are future work. The available datasets contain visible labels, so a future experiment must actively enforce label hiding.
Chapter 9 · Practice

Now you’re ready to explore.

For any dataset, work through these questions in order. Keep training-only exploration separate from held-out score analysis when designing a fair experiment.

1. Understand the assay

What protein and property are measured? Which score direction is better? What are the conditions and units?

2. Inspect the rows

How many variants, missing values, duplicate sequences, and sequence lengths are there? What does each column mean?

3. Describe variation

What reference is used? How many substitutions does each variant have? Are there distinct proteins or variable sites?

4. Study scores and splits

Plot score distributions and mutation counts. How were train and test chosen? What may be hidden from the optimizer?

5. Translate findings into an experiment

Pick a question, a baseline, an evaluation plan, a realistic budget, and a label-hiding protocol. Record caveats before interpreting results.

A suggested learning order

First: inspect GFP mutations and scores. Next: compare GB1 train and test. Then: investigate Hydro by protein. Finally, verify the Amylase and IRED assay details before choosing an experimental setup.

A small working vocabulary.

Amino acidA building block of a protein, represented by a letter.
VariantA protein sequence considered relative to a reference.
AssayAn experiment that measures a property.
Fitness / target / labelThe measured or processed score used as an outcome, in this context.
Wild typeA reference sequence, often naturally occurring; exact meaning depends on the dataset.
SplitA division of examples into roles such as training, validation, and testing.
GeneralizationHow well a model works on examples outside those used to fit it.
Proxy / oracleA proxy is a substitute prediction; an oracle provides the score when queried in a simulated experiment.
EpistasisWhen the effect of a mutation depends on other mutations.
